What her breakup interview teaches us about love bombing, control, and finding your way back to yourself

Anupama Parameswaran's recent interview about her breakup pulled in a lot of eyes not because of who her ex was (she never said), but because of what she said: "I don't even know how I got here. I used to be so confident." She described nearly two years of what she called narcissistic abuse behind one cryptic Instagram post about letting go of dead ends, and admitted her body reacted before her mind did the first time she heard someone else describe the same pattern.

That line is one I hear in my office constantly, from clients who walk in confused and apologetic, sometimes still defending the person who hurt them. This blog is for them. By the time someone recognizes they're in a narcissistically abusive relationship, they've usually already lost years not just time, but weight, friendships, savings, and the shape of who they used to be.

I want to walk through this properly. Not as a checklist, but as a story, because that's how it actually happens. It rarely feels like abuse while it's happening. It feels like love that keeps almost slipping away, and you keep trying harder to hold onto it.

## Phase One: Love Bombing ( The Hook )

It rarely starts with cruelty. It starts with the best attention you've ever received.

A client of mine I'll call Indhu met a man who messaged her good morning every single day for three months. He remembered she liked Hershey's Choco Chip and showed up with a tin of it on their third date. He told her within six weeks that she was "different from anyone he'd ever met," that he'd "never felt this understood.", “he can be childish and himself”, “ that he cannot believe someone like her would ever be his” accepted He planned elaborate future plans early trips, a house, even marriage long before it made sense to.

This is love bombing, and it works precisely because it isn't fake affection layered over a plan. In the moment, it often feels real to the narcissist too they are genuinely high on the supply of admiration and possibility a new partner represents. The intensity isn't romance; it's velocity. It's designed, consciously or not, to bypass your normal pace of trust-building. Real intimacy is built slowly, through small tests of reliability over time. Love bombing skips that it front-loads intensity so that by the time inconsistency shows up, you already feel "in too deep" to walk away also involved with his parents could be your family too to call it off when things begin to feel overwhelming.

**What it often looks like day to day:**

- Constant texting, share me your live location wanting to know where you are "because I miss you and want to make sure your safe," not because of suspicion yet
- Mirroring: suddenly loving your favorite food, your hobbies, your values
- Fast-tracked labels "soulmate," "the one" within weeks
- Grand gestures that feel disproportionate to how long you've known each other
- Isolating language disguised as devotion: "I don't need anyone else when I have you."

## Phase Two: The Cracks Begin Devaluation

Somewhere around the point where the relationship becomes stable a few months in, sometimes after moving in together, marriage, or a baby something shifts. The same person who once said your laugh/way of talking was his favorite sound now says you laugh "too loud" or you talk too sweet in front of his friends or family.

This is the devaluation phase, and it's rarely a single dramatic moment. It's small, deniable, and it accumulates.

**Real-life patterns I hear constantly:**

- **The compliment isn't:** *"You looked so much better in traditional wear than modern outfit."* Said casually, almost affectionately, so you're not sure if you should be hurt.
- **The public correction:** At a dinner with friends, he corrects a story you're telling not the facts, but your delivery. "That's not really how it happened, babe." Everyone laughs. You feel small and can't say why.
- **The withdrawal test:** Warmth one day, coldness the next, with no acknowledged cause. You spend your energy trying to figure out what you did.
- **Moving goalposts:** You finally ask, let's shift to the house we were planning or trip that you were talking about or the promotion he said would make you proud — and now the complaint is that you ask too much and "care more about what the world is doing and not what we really want."

This inconsistency isn't random. Psychologically, it's what creates a trauma bond. Tthe same intermittent-reinforcement pattern that makes slot machines addictive. When love is unpredictable, your brain doesn't give up chasing it; it chases it harder. You start working for the good version of them like it's a prize you have to re-earn.

## Why It's Often an Empath Who Gets Chosen

This part matters, and it's not a coincidence, and it's not your fault for being kind.

Narcissistic personality patterns are often rooted in a fragile, hollow sense of self not confidence, but a desperate compensation for its absence. Grandiosity is armor over emptiness, not the opposite of it.

An empathetic partner is, to that hollowness, incredibly valuable someone who listens deeply, forgives easily, gives the benefit of the doubt, and reflects back exactly the admiration the narcissist is starved for.

But there's a crueler layer too: an empath who is genuinely secure, warm, and frankly "out of their league" is also a threat. Their independence, their friendships, their competence at work, all remind the narcissist of what they lack. So the very qualities that attracted them become the target.

Controlling and diminishing an empath isn't just about supply, it's about closing the gap. If they can shrink you down, they no longer feel outmatched by you.

This is why victims are so often told, confusingly, "you think you're better than me" right before being belittled for something they were genuinely good at.

## How Control Actually Gets Built Piece by Piece

Control in these relationships is rarely one big rule. It's dozens of small ones that add up to a cage you don't remember walking into.

**Finances.** It might start caring "let us start a joint account - your savings will remain an RBI reserve for us." Over time, you stop knowing the account balances. You need to ask before buying anything for yourself, while unexplained expenses on his side go unquestioned. Some victims I've worked with had every paycheck routed into a joint account they had no visibility into; others were pressured to contribute to the partners growth which quietly erased their financial independence and, with it, their exit route.

**Who you speak to.** Not an outright "you can't see your friends" that would be obvious. Instead: *"Why do you need to go out when you have me?"* or a sulky silence every time you mention plans, until you simply stop making them to avoid the friction. Friends stop being invited "for peace." Family visits get sabotaged by conveniently timed arguments right before you're due to leave.

**Daily choices.** What you wear ("are you really going out like that?"), what you eat ("you're getting a wafer again?"), how you spend your evenings, even how you parent all quietly steered, framed always as concern, never as control.

**Your work.** This one sneaks in through the mood. A bad day at his job becomes a bad night for you criticism, coldness, a fight that starts over nothing. Over time you start walking on eggshells around his professional frustrations, managing his moods as an unpaid, unacknowledged job on top of your own.

## What Happens to You

This is the part people underestimate. It's not just emotional, it's physical, visible, measurable.

Clients describe losing significant weight without trying, or the opposite comfort eating in private because food is one of the only things that still feels like theirs. Skin "glow" the things people used to compliment fade, because chronic cortisol from walking on eggshells does that to a body. Ambitions quietly shrink; the promotion you'd have chased two years ago now feels like too much effort, too much risk of upsetting the balance at home.

And then there's the public humiliation, delivered with a smile: "She used to be so fun before she got so sensitive,"she is all talks, knows nothing in action” said in front of friends, met with laughter, leaving you to either play along or seem humorless. Over months, this erodes your credibility with the very people who could have helped you see clearly.

The cruelest part is most victims don't clock the decline while it's happening. It's only in hindsight an old photo, an old voice memo where you sound like a different person that the loss becomes visible.

## When You Try to Leave

If devaluation is chapter two, this is where the story gets frightening. Nothing exposes the truth of a narcissistic partner faster than a serious attempt to leave.

**Rage.** Not the cold sulk from before real fury. Yelling, sometimes throwing things, sometimes threats about custody, money, reputation, or worse.

**Silence and isolation as punishment.** Days/months of being frozen out entirely, treated as if you don't exist, only to be met later with a small kindness that resets the cycle.

**Hoovering.** A sudden return of the love-bombing script flowers, tears, promises to change, therapy appointments booked and never attended, or the classic: reappearing right when you've started to feel steady again, or the moment you post something showing you're doing well without them.

**Running back to an ex, or finding a replacement fast.** This isn't heartbreak it's supply management. Losing you as a source of admiration and control is intolerable, and a replacement (real or performed) is often lined up with startling speed, partly to punish you, partly to reassure themselves they haven't lost their power.

**Boundary violations don't stop at "no."** This is a hard truth I say gently but plainly in session: many victims describe sexual encounters that happened not from desire but from exhaustion, guilt, or fear of another blow-up coercion dressed as intimacy. If this is part of your story, it counts as abuse, even if it never looked like the version of assault you'd imagined.

## Why People Stay And Why That Makes Complete Sense

I never want a client to leave my office feeling stupid for having stayed. Staying makes sense when you understand the mechanics:

- **Trauma bonding** means the good moments feel more intense *because* of the bad ones relief itself becomes addictive.
- **Hope is doing enormous work.** You remember who they were during love bombing and believe that person is still in there, just needs the right conditions to come back.
- **Isolation removes your reality checks.** Fewer friends means fewer people to say "this isn't normal."
- **Financial dependence** can make leaving feel materially impossible, not just emotionally hard.
- **Self-worth erosion** means you start to believe the criticisms that you're "too sensitive," "too much," or that no one else would want you anyway.

None of this is weakness. It's what a well-functioning nervous system does under prolonged, unpredictable stress.

## Recognizing the Pattern Core Traits

If you're trying to work out whether this describes your relationship, here's what I'd have you sit with:

- A grandiose self-image alongside an inability to tolerate any criticism, however gentle
- Charm that's switched on and off depending on audience and usefulness
- Chronic lack of accountability mistakes are always someone else's fault
- Contempt disguised as jokes, "just being honest," or concern
- A need to win every disagreement, even ones that don't need winning
- Jealousy of your success, framed as "just wanting more time with you"
- Explosive anger or icy withdrawal when they don't get their way often both, on rotation
- A private self that contradicts the public performance

## Getting Out, and Getting Back to Yourself

If any of this has felt uncomfortably familiar, here's what I'd tell you in session:

**Start documenting quietly** messages, incidents, financial records, before you say anything about leaving. Clarity now protects you later.

**Rebuild one connection at a time.** You don't need your whole old life back overnight. One honest phone call to a friend you pulled away from is enough to start.

**Get your own financial footing**, even small steps an account only you can access, a copy of important documents somewhere safe.

**Expect the hoovering, and decide in advance how you'll respond to it.** Deciding in a calm moment is far easier than deciding mid-flowers-on-the-doorstep.

**Work with a therapist who understands coercive control specifically** — not all couples counselling is appropriate here, and in fact it can sometimes be misused by the narcissistic partner as a stage. Individual trauma-informed therapy is usually the safer starting point.

**Self-advocacy is not selfishness.** This is the line I repeat most in my practice: prioritizing your own wellbeing after a relationship like this isn't you becoming "difficult" or "cold" it's you recovering the boundary that should have been there from the start. The version of you that gets to want things, say no, and take up space isn't a harder version of you. It's the real one, finally allowed back.

If you recognized yourself in this even a little please know that clarity about the pattern is often the first real turning point. You're not imagining it, and you're not too sensitive. If you're currently in a relationship where you feel unsafe, a domestic violence helpline can help you think through both emotional and physical safety planning, even if you're not ready to leave yet.
